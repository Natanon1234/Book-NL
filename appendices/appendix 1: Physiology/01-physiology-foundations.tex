\section{Introduction}
\textbf{Balance}\index{physiological balance} is at the core of physiology, from pressure between the \textbf{fluid compartments}\index{body fluid compartments} to blood concentration and acidity to urine output. The human body aims to strike a delicate balance between all elements internal to external, which requires numerous mechanisms to control each parameter in the body. At the microscopic level, cells maintain a balance between their internal and outer environments across a \textbf{cell membrane}\index{cell membrane}, which serves to regulate the micro environment to allow for cellular activity. If this balance is disrupted, damage of the cell and/or degradation of its functions occurs.

\subsection{Structure \& Composition of the Cell Membrane}
\textbf{Cell membranes}\index{cell membrane} are a key part of all cells: they separate the intracellular\index{intracellular environment} and extracellular\index{extracellular environment} environments, allowing cells to regulate their cytosol\index{cytosol}, such as controlling solute concentration and internal pH\index{pH}. This is essential for the performance of functions within the cell, as well as intracellular signaling\index{intracellular signaling}. However, cell membranes are not perfectly impermeable barriers: substances can cross either into or out of the cell through multiple mechanisms, either regulated or unregulated. These mechanisms exist to allow cells to interact with the outside world, acquiring nutrients and excreting waste, as well as sending and receiving signals. While some molecules are able to effectively permeate through the cell membrane, most require some form of assistance to cross the \textbf{lipid bilayer}\index{lipid bilayer}. For these molecules, both energy-expending and energy-independent mechanisms exist. These mechanisms, as well as their controls are discussed further in this chapter.



\section{Passive transport}
A major mechanism for the movement of molecules is \textbf{diffusion}\index{diffusion}, which involves the movement of small particles following the concentration gradient\index{concentration gradient}, which refers to the difference in concentration between the two sides of a permeable membrane. \textbf{Passive transport}\index{passive transport} does not require energy expenditure from the cell, as the concentration or electrochemical gradient\index{electrochemical gradient} drives equalization. Passive transport can be achieved through both membrane diffusion (where small lipid soluble molecules move through the cell membrane) and \textbf{facilitated diffusion}\index{facilitated diffusion}.

Facilitated (passive) diffusion mainly refers to \textbf{channel-mediated transport}\index{channel-mediated transport} or transport via carrier proteins\index{carrier protein}. Channel-mediated transport is typically used for substances such as ions\index{ion}, which are not able to independently cross the cell membrane effectively. In channel-mediated transport, channel proteins form aqueous pores, which allow substances to diffuse across. For instance, K$^{+}$ channels allow the flow of potassium ions. Alternatively, in carrier-mediated transport\index{carrier-mediated transport}, a carrier protein binds to a substance and undergoes conformational changes to transport specific substances (such as glucose) across the cell membrane.

\section{Active transport}
Another major mechanism for particles is \textbf{active transport}\index{active transport}, which is a broad category of mechanisms where cellular energy (in the form of \textbf{ATP}\index{ATP}) is used, either directly or indirectly, to transport substances. The major mechanisms for this are through \textbf{direct active transport}\index{direct active transport} and \textbf{secondary active transport}\index{secondary active transport}. These mechanisms differ depending on if transport directly utilizes ATP to mobilize the target substrate.

Direct active transport refers to the usage of \textbf{ATP-dependent transporters}\index{ATP-dependent transporter}, often referred to as pumps\index{pump}, which actively bind to the target substrate and undergo conformational changes which result in a specific substance being transported against the concentration gradient directly (see: Na-K ATPase [todo]). This type of transport establishes a potential gradient\index{potential gradient} within the cell, which may be utilized in other processes. For instance, in neurons, the gradient of sodium and potassium is equalized during signaling, resulting in the subsequent release of calcium and neurotransmitters (see: depolarization of the neuron [todo]).
