\subsection{Cell membrane}
Cell membranes are a key part of all cells: they seperate the intracellular and extracellular environments, allowing cells to regulate their cytosol, such as controlling solute concentration and internal pH. This is essential for the performance of functions within the cell, as well as intracellular signalling. However, cell membranes are not perfectly impermiable barriers: substances can cross either into or out of the cell through multiple mechanisms, either regulated or unregulated. These mechanisms exist to allow cells to interact with the outside world, acuqiring nutrients and excreting waste, as well as sending and recieving signals.

\subsection{Passive transport}
A major mechanism for the movement of molecules is diffusion\index{diffusion}, which involves the movement of small particles following the concentration gradient\index{concentration gradient}, which refers to the difference in concentration between the two sides of a permeable membrane. Passive transport\index{passive transport} does not require energy expenditure from the cell, as the concentration or electrochemical gradient drives equalization. Passive transport can be achieved through both membrane diffusion (where small lipid soluble molecules move through the cell membrane) and facilitated diffusion\index{facilitated diffusion}.

Facilitated (passive) diffusion mainly refers to channel-mediated transport or transport via carrier proteins. Channel-mediated transport is typically used for substances such as ions, which are not able to independently cross the cell membrane effectively. In channel-mediated transport, channel proteins form aqueous pores, which allow substances to diffuse across. For instance, K$^{+}$ channels allow the flow of potassium ions. Alternatively, in carrier-mediated transport, a carrier protein binds to a substance and undergoes conformational changes to transport specific substances (such as glucose) across the cell membrane.

\subsection{Active transport}
Another major mechanism for particles is active transport, which is a broad category of mechanisms where cellular energy (in the form of ATP) is used, either directly or indirectly, to transport substances. The major mechanisms for this are through direct active transport and secondary active transport. These mechanisms differ depending on if transport directly utilizes ATP to mobilize the target substrate.

Direct acitve transport refers to the usage of ADP-dependant transporters, often refered to as pupmps, which actively bind to the target substrate and undergo conformational changes which result in a specific substance being transported \emph{against} the concentration gradient directly (see: Na-K ATPase [todo]). This type of transport establishes a potential gradient within the cell, which may be utilized in other processes. For instance, in neurons, the gradient of sodium and potassium is equalized during signalling, resulting in the subsequent release of calcium and neurotransmitters (see: depolarization of the neuron [todo])

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{B1: Cross-cutting foundations} — energy metabolism (B1.1.6); cell structure, signal transduction, cellular homeostasis, and basic tissue components (B1.2.1, B1.2.2, B1.2.6, B1.2.8); exercise and sleep physiology (B1.7.3--B1.7.4).
  \item \textbf{B2.1: Hematopoietic and lymphoreticular systems} — development and perinatal changes; bone marrow, lymph nodes, thymus, and spleen; production and function of erythrocytes, hemoglobin, O$_2$/CO$_2$ transport, leukocytes, platelets, coagulation and fibrinolysis; repair and life-stage changes.
  \item \textbf{B3.1: Central and peripheral nervous systems} — development; spinal cord, brain stem, brain, sensory and motor systems, autonomic nervous system, and peripheral nerves; neuronal excitability, neurotransmitters and receptors, glia and myelin; brain homeostasis, blood-brain barrier, and cerebrospinal fluid; life-stage changes.
  \item \textbf{B4.1: Skin and related connective tissue} — development and structure; barrier function, thermal regulation, and eccrine function; repair and changes across life stages and ethnicity; skin defenses and normal flora.
  \item \textbf{B5.1: Musculoskeletal system} — development and structure; bones, joints, tendons, and skeletal muscle; exercise and physical conditioning; repair and life-stage changes.
  \item \textbf{B6.1: Respiratory system} — development; airways, mechanics and regulation of breathing; lung ventilation, perfusion, and gas exchange; pleura and upper airways; surfactant and alveolar structure; repair, life-stage changes, pulmonary defenses, and normal flora.
  \item \textbf{B7.1: Cardiovascular system} — development; chambers, valves, cardiac cycle, mechanics, heart sounds, and conduction; hemodynamics and circulation in specific vascular beds; heart muscle, endothelium, vascular smooth muscle, microcirculation, and lymph flow; neural and hormonal regulation, including responses to posture, exercise, and tissue metabolism; life-stage changes.
  \item \textbf{B8.1: Gastrointestinal system} — development; alimentary canal, liver and biliary system, salivary glands, and exocrine pancreas; motility, digestion, and absorption; neural and hormonal regulation; gastrointestinal, pancreatic, and hepatic secretions and liver metabolism; repair, life-stage changes, defenses, and normal flora.
  \item \textbf{B9.1: Renal and urinary system} — development; kidneys, ureters, bladder, and urethra; glomerular filtration and hemodynamics; tubular reabsorption and secretion; urine concentration and dilution; acid-base and body-fluid homeostasis; micturition; renal metabolism and hormones; life-stage changes.
  \item \textbf{B10.1: Reproductive and perinatal physiology} — development and perinatal changes; female and male reproductive structure and function; menstrual cycle, puberty, menopause, spermatogenesis, intercourse, and orgasm; pregnancy, labor, delivery, puerperium, and lactation; hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis and reproductive hormones; defenses and life-stage changes.
  \item \textbf{B11.1: Endocrine system} — development; hypothalamus, pituitary, thyroid, parathyroids, adrenals, pancreatic islets, gonads, and adipose tissue; hormone synthesis, secretion, action, and metabolism, including peptide, steroid, thyroid, and catecholamine hormones and the renin-angiotensin system; life-stage changes.
\end{itemize}
