\section{Introduction to Medical Virology}

\marginnote{\begin{kaobox}[frametitle={Origins of selected virus names},backgroundcolor=RoyalBlue!12!White,frametitlebackgroundcolor=RoyalBlue!25!White]\label{box:virus-naming}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textit{Picornaviridae}\index{Picornaviridae} are small RNA viruses; the name combines ``pico'' (small) and RNA.
  \item \textit{Togaviridae}\index{Togaviridae} have a membrane envelope; the name derives from Latin \textit{toga}, meaning `cloak'.
  \item Reoviruses\index{reovirus} are named from ``respiratory, enteric, orphan''; they have segmented double-stranded RNA genomes and do not use reverse transcription.
  \item Poxviruses\index{poxvirus} are named for pock-like lesions; the family includes variola virus\index{variola virus}, which causes smallpox.
  \item Adenoviruses\index{adenovirus} were first isolated from adenoid tissue; the family is named \textit{Adenoviridae}\index{Adenoviridae}.
\end{itemize}
\end{kaobox}}

\textbf{Viruses}\index{virus} may be defined as microscopic, acellular, \textbf{obligate intracellular infectious agents}\index{obligate intracellular agents} that replicate only within host cells. \textbf{Viral replication}\index{viral replication} depends on host-cell resources and viral components to produce progeny\index{progeny}, which are referred to as virions\index{virion} (they are the extracellular form of a virus).\footnote{They do not reproduce independently or by binary fission, and hence are not typically considered living organisms.} The majority of viruses cause disease, but viruses vary in their interactions with and evasion of host defenses. Medically important viruses often share characteristics, including (1) the ability to infect susceptible cells\index{succeptible cells} and produce viral particles; (2) interactions with host immune defenses, including mechanisms of \textbf{immune evasion}\index{immune evasion}; and (3) \textbf{transmission between hosts}\index{viral transmission} by defined means, often through protective barriers (e.g., contact, respiratory droplets or aerosols, fecal-oral transmission, vectors, or other routes). Some routes are depicted in figure~\ref{fig:transmissionroute}.

Viruses consist of genetic material (either \textbf{DNA}\index{viral DNA}, deoxyribonucleic acid, or \textbf{RNA}\index{viral RNA}, ribonucleic acid) enclosed in a \textbf{protein capsid}\index{capsid}.\footnote{One subunit of a protein capsid is referred to as the \textbf{capsomere}\index{capsomere}.} Some viruses possess a lipid envelope\index{viral envelope} surrounding their capsid. These viruses are referred to as \textbf{enveloped viruses}. Alternately, non-enveloped viruses are \textbf{naked viruses}\index{naked virus}. Both enveloped and naked viruses may present spikes on the surface of their virion: for naked viruses, these spikes present on the protein capsid, while for enveloped viruses, they present on the outermost envelope.

Classification of viruses utilizes factors such as genome type and replication strategy (following the \textbf{Baltimore classification}), capsid symmetry, and the presence or absence of an envelope. Notably, formal classification systems are also used in practice. Virus names may reflect characteristics such as size, morphology, target infection site, patient symptoms. Refer to Box~\ref{box:virus-naming} for examples of nomenclature for selected common viruses.

\section{The Classification of Viruses}

Four commonly used descriptors for virus classification are (1) genetic composition; (2) capsid symmetry\index{capsid symmetry}\footnote{Surprisingly not much different from a `Dynamite' candy with its soft chocolate core; you can tell I am hungry.}; (3) the presence or absence of a lipid envelope; and (4) the dimensions of the virion\index{virion} or capsid. These features help describe and compare viruses, but formal taxonomy also considers genetic and evolutionary relationships.

Capsid morphology provides a useful structural description. Common forms include helical and icosahedral capsids, while some virions have more complex structures. Envelope status is a separate feature: enveloped virions also have a capsid beneath their lipid membrane.

We first focus on classification based on the outer shell of a virus. The outer layer protects the viral genome and assists in delivery to target cells through \textbf{viral attachment proteins (VAPs)}\index{viral attachment protein}.\footnote{Antibodies that block VAPs can prevent viral entry and neutralize some viruses.} The viral genome is enclosed in a protein coat (capsid); enveloped viruses also have a lipid envelope surrounding the capsid.

Non-enveloped viruses (\textbf{naked viruses})\index{naked virus} are generally more resistant than enveloped viruses to detergents, drying, acids, and bile due to their rigid protein capsids. This allows many of them to be transmitted by the fecal---oral route\index{fecal-oral transmission}.

On the other hand, \textbf{enveloped viruses}\index{enveloped virus}, because of their lipid envelopes, are more susceptible to drying, detergents, and solvents. Therefore, they are typically transmitted through fluids (including bodily secretions, blood, and tissue), and many do not survive in the gastrointestinal tract.

\section{Viral Infection}

\subsection{Staging of Viral Infections}\label{stagingvirus}
Viral infections are either productive\index{productive infection} or abortive\index{abortive infection} (failed infections), depending on both factors determined by the host cell and virus. For an infection to succeed, the cell must be susceptible and permissive\footnote{After all, it should come as no surprise that a virus must first be able to infect the cell, as well as replicate within it to infect the host. Otherwise, an infection will become abortive, which means that new viral progeny is produced.}. A \textbf{susceptible cell}\index{succeptible cell (to virus)} is a cell which expresses receptors that may bind viral proteins and allow entry, while a permissive cell\index{permissive cell (to virus)} contains the intracellular substances required for a virus to proliferate.

A successful infection may be classified further based on the duration. Infections may either be acute or chronic, and chronic infections may be further classified based on the presence or absence of a detectable viral load. A latent infection\index{latent infection} presents without active viral load, but with the ability to re-infect if the host immunity drops. On the other hand, chronic infections\index{chronic infection} present with a persistent (and typically reduced viral load compared to the acute phase of infection). Therefore, chronic infections often present as asymptomatic. Refer to~\ref{c2-viral_stage} for more details.

\subsection{Pathogenesis of Viral Infection}

The human body typically has a number of innate and adaptive defenses against infections (covered in:~\ref{immune_respond_chapter}) that my serve to immediately prevent or abort an infection. These defenses serve to disrupt the infective process of pathogens. Infections occur when these mechanisms are weakened or bypassed (such as immunocompromised or breaches of the cutaneous barrier). The distribution of a viral infection can best be described as either localized or systemic. Localized infections\index{localized infection} refer to colonization at and around the site of infection, while disseminated infections\index{disseminated infection} refer to those which have spread to distant sites.\footnote{This typically occurs through viremia\index{viremia} which refers to the presence of a virus within the bloodstream, which may occur transiently.} The route of entry for viruses governs (to some extent) the patterns of infection and subsequent pathology that are expected. Examples of disseminated spread include: Invasion of sub-epithelial and lymphatic tissue, vascular endothelial invasion, persistent viremia and macrophage or nerve infection/spread.

The major barrier mechanisms of the immune system against viruses are:\footnote{refer to~\ref{immune_respond_chapter} for details}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Anatomical and chemical barriers --- barriers that inactivate viruses or prevent penetration of viruses into susceptible cells. This includes physical barriers and secreted substances such as mucus, saliva, and defensins, as well as physical barriers such as skin (the majority of infections stop at this step).
  \item Intrinsic barriers --- barriers which prevent viruses from taking root in host cells. This includes cellular mechanisms such as apoptosis, autophagy and interferons.
  \item Innate immunity --- the immune system's immediate and nonspecific response to pathogens through recognition of pathogenic factors (often pathogen associated molecular patterns). This involves phagocytes, NK cells, antigen presenting cells (APCs) and neutrophils.
  \item Acquired immunity --- the immune system's customized response for each pathogen presented by APCs. This involves the production of antibodies by immune cells and targeted attack of the pathogen.
\end{itemize}

For a viral infection to take root, the virus must: (1) have a mechanism of bypassing host immunity; (2) be present in an amount exceeding the inoculum dose; (3) cells must both be susceptible and permissive (see figure~\ref{stagingvirus}) and (4) host immunity must be insufficient to defend against the infection. If one or more of these factors are not present, viral infection will not occur.

The viral (infective) titer of a virus\index{viral titer} is a measure used to establish infectivity and it is defined as the minimum inoculum of a virus required to establish an infection. Various experimental assays may be used to make this determination.\footnote{Hence, the specific value varies by assay platform.} Example platforms include: tissue culture (TCID\textsubscript{50} and PFU) and animal culture (ID\textsubscript{50} and LD\textsubscript{50}).

\subsection{Viral Dissemination}
\label{sub_virus_disseminate}
Once a virus has established an infection at the site of entry, it often spreads to other sites. This is known as \textbf{dissemination}\index{dissemination}. It involves movement of viruses to lymph nodes and subsequently to the bloodstream, which is defined as \textbf{primary viremia}\index{primary viremia}.\footnote{This stage is clinically silent.} Once a virus has established primary viremia, it may infect organs, including blood vessels, muscles and other organs. As a virus sheds from these organs, it may establish \textbf{secondary viremia}\index{secondary viremia} which leads to further transmission. At any point during this process, the virus may be shed from reproductive sites to other hosts. Major sites of shedding include the skin, mucous membranes, lungs, kidneys and gastrointestinal tract. Refer to~\ref{c2-dissemination} For an overview.

Viruses within the bloodstream may either be present in the plasma or contained within/adsorbed to the cellular components of blood (leukocytes, platelets, erythrocytes, lymphocytes, monocytes). Macrophages actively reduce the viral load, which results in the natural decay of infectivity.\footnote{If macrophages are impaired, virus removal suffers greatly.}

The respiratory tract --- spread of a respiratory virus throughout the respiratory tract and potentially into the sinuses, middle ear or alveoli is achieved through release of viral particles into the mucus layer coating respiratory epithelium. This propagation mechanism is enhanced by sneezing, coughing or inhalation of mucosal secretions.

Skin --- an initial infection at the basal lamina of the epidermis by a virus moves in two directions: lateral spread at the basal lamina, and vertically (as the basal lamina replaces shedded skin, and as the virus moves into subepithelial tissue). Dissemination may occur into lymphatic and blood vessels or nerves.\footnote{Many factors can restrict viral spread in the epithelium: (1)  viruses may bud on the apical surface of epithelium; (2) viruses may be unable to cross the intact basement membrane; (3) cells may not be permissive; (4) neutralizing antibodies may exist; (5) viruses may be temperature sensitive and (6) proteolytic cleavage of viral proteins may be only in specific tissue.}

Select mechanisms of viral dissemination:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Lymphatic dissemination\index{lymphatic dissemination} --- invasion of subepithelial tissue induces phagocytosis. These viruses are then able to replicate in monocytes (particularly macrophages) and lymphocytes, bypassing immune surveillance. These viruses are then able to infect cells of the lymphatic system and induce local or systemic dissemination.
  \item Vascular dissemination\index{vascular dissemination} --- the virus breaches the endothelial barrier and disseminates in the bloodstream. Virions may extravasate by transcytosis, monocyte/lymphocyte trafficking or infecting endothelial cells.
  \item Macrophage-based dissemination\index{macrophage-based dissemination} --- the virus resides and replicates in macrophages such as Kupffer cells (at hepatic sinusoids).\footnote{Macrophages may also be infected and not disseminate some viruses.}
  \item Nervous dissemination\index{macrophage-based dissemination}\footnote{Nervous as in nervous system!} --- after a breach of the epithelial barrier, neuro\-invasive,\footnote{Viruses which may enter the CNS after infecting a peripheral site. ``How easily can I infect nervous tissue''?} neurotrophic\footnote{Viruses which may infect neurons. Typically after neural/hematogenous dissemination. ``I can live in nervous tissue''.} and neurovirulent\footnote{Viruses which may cause disease of nervous tissue. ``How much damage can I cause''?} viruses may infect and spread throughout the nervous system in an anterograde and/or retrograde manner to infect nervous tissue or other sites (anterograde means from cell body to axon terminals, retrograde means from axon terminals to cell body). For instance, HSV replicates and establishes latency within sensory ganglions.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Mechanisms of Viral Pathology}

Viral pathology\index{viral pathology} is typically divided into cytopathology\index{cytopathology} and immunopathology\index{immunopathology}. Cytopathology refers to pathology which occurs as a result of viral replication, while immunopathology refers to damage caused by the immune system in response to pathogens.

Major determining factors of cytopathology are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Efficiency of viral replication --- factors such as the optimum temperature for the virus and the permissiveness of the cell.
  \item Cytotoxic viral proteins --- effects from: (1) proteins that inhibit macromolecule synthesis; (2) accumulation of viral products or altered cell metabolism.
  \item Immune evasion ---the ability of a virus to evade nonspecific (interferons, NK cells, macrophages) and specific (T-cell, antibody) antiviral responses.
\end{itemize}

Major causes of immunopathology are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Interferons/cytokines --- flu-like symptoms.
  \item T-cell responses --- cytotoxicity, inflammation.
  \item Antibodies --- compliment, antibody-dependant cellular cytotoxicity, immune complexes
  \item Other --- other inflammatory responses, cytokine storm or immunosuppression.\footnote{Immunosuppression is quite specific to HIV infections.}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Manifestation of Viral Disease}

Virus infection is divided into three phases: (1) the incubation phase\index{incubation (of virus)}, which presents as either asymptomatic or with prodromal symptoms; (2) the symptomatic phase\index{symptomatic phase (of virus)}, in which the virus causes damage to the host cell and potentially causes systemic effects; (3) the convalescent phase\index{convalescent phase} of a virus, where damage is repaired and immune memory is established. While the duration and timing of each phase may vary between viruses and hosts, all stages are typically present in viral infection. Select infections and their tissue localizations are presented in figure~\ref{fig:pathogen_sites}.

The incubation period begins with entry of the virus into the body (\textbf{acquisition}\index{acquisition (of virus)}) and subsequent invasion at the primary site.\footnote{This typically activates innate immune responses.} After a period of replication, it may spread to distant sites, which is referred to as dissemination (refer to~\ref{sub_virus_disseminate} for information on dissemination).

In the symptomatic phase, the virus establishes an infection in the target, which results in signs specific to each virus. The site of infection is often dependant on the \textbf{trophism}\index{viral trophism} of the virus, which refers to the site that is preferentially infected by the virus. In this phase, tissue. In this phase, immunopathogenesis may occur as a result of immune response to the infection. Additionally, viral transmission between susceptible hosts may also occur, as viral shedding (refer to~\ref{c2-dissemination} for details on transmission).


In the convalescent phase\index{convalensent phase} of viral infection, the body repairs damage from previous phases and subsequently develops immune memory. Alternatively, persistent or chronic infection may still be present.

\subsection{Epidemiology of Viral Infection}

\textbf{Epidemiology}\index{epidemiology} is the study of the distribution, dynamics and determinants of disease within the human population. Many factors influence viral transmissibility and the effectiveness of viral transmission. These factors are typically divided into into pathogen and host factors. Pathogen factors are the factors which are inherent to a particular pathogen, such as the characteristics of the virus and virulence, while host factors include immunity, behaviour as well as the environment and ecology. Important factors influencing viral spread include the presence of asymptomatic transmission and a susceptible population, as well as the presence of control mechanisms.\footnote{These mechanisms allow for the reduced spread of viruses, and include quarantine, vector elimination, immunization, vaccination along with treatment and education.} A summarized list of select key terms in epidemiology follow.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Prevalence\index{prevalence} --- refers to the number of infected individuals as a proportion of the given population (e.g., 20\%).
  \item Attack rate\index{attack rate} --- also known as the \textbf{incidence} this statistic refers to the number of events over a period of time. It is usually expressed as cases per standard population size.
  \item Seroprevalence\index{Seroprevalence} --- refers to the proportion of individuals with immunity against a given agent in a population.
\end{itemize}

Many survival mechanisms exist for viruses within nature and the host. These mechanisms include ability to reinfect hosts, persistent infection, zoonotic persistence and animal reservoirs. Notably, \textbf{zoonosis}\index{zoonosis} refers to the transmission of a pathogen between animals into humans. This can either be through contact or bites. If a zoonotic virus is able to transmit between humans, we may refer to it as vertebrate-vertebrate transmission, while zoonotic viruses without the ability to transmit between humans are viruses with a vertebrate reservoir.

The spread of viruses throughout the population may be defined by geographic spread. Endemic disease is defined as disease which occurs at a low but stable frequency in an area, while sporadic diseases occur frequently and show a scattered geographic distribution. On the other hand, epidemic disease describes the rapid spread of viruses within a population, and pandemic is the occurrence of a disease in more than one country concurrently.

Epidemiology provides many benefits, including: (1) the identification of outbreak sources; (2) information on modes of transmission; (3) the roles of viruses in disease etiology; (4) the understanding of virus-environment interactions and environmental determinants; (5) the implementation of prevention strategies and (6) disease controls.

\subsection{Emerging Infectious Diseases}

Emerging infectious diseases\index{emerging infectious diseases}(EIDs) refers to the appearance of new pathogens as well as the increasing incidence of previously well-controlled pathogens that continue to evolve and spread. Generally, the pattern begins with the emergence of a new agent, followed by an abrupt increase in illnesses which is caused by the agent invading a susceptible host population.

Contributing factors to the prevalence of EIDs include:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Environmental changes such as deforestation, damming, ecosystem shifts, floods, droughts or famine.
  \item Human behaviors, such as sex, drug use and outdoor recreation.
  \item Socioeconomic and demographic phenomena (war, poverty, population growth/migration, urban decay).
  \item Travel or commerce.
  \item Food production and supply chains, changes in food processing and packaging.
  \item Healthcare, such as new medical devices, blood transfusions, organ/tissue transplantation, immunosuppressants, widespread use of antibiotics.
  \item Public health measures, including sanitation, vector control, prevention programs and personnel.
\end{itemize}

EIDs may be classified into 4 main stages according to their ability to infect animal and human hosts:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Stage I --- refers to viruses that may only infect animals.
  \item Stage II --- refers to viruses which infect animals that may spread to humans, but is unable to spread between humans.
  \item Stage III --- refers to viruses from animals that have a limited spread between humans.
  \item Stage IV --- refers to viruses with the clear ability to spread effectively both between humans and between animals.
  \item Stage V --- refers to viruses which exclusively spread between humans.
\end{itemize}
